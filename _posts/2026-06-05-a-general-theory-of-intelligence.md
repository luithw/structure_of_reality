# A General Theory of Intelligence

For much of the history of artificial intelligence, the field has been haunted by the phrase *general intelligence*.

We want to know what would make a machine generally intelligent rather than narrowly competent. We want an agent that can learn many tasks, transfer knowledge across domains, reason under uncertainty, use tools, adapt to novelty, and act in the world without being rebuilt for every new situation. This is usually framed as the search for a theory of general intelligence.

That is an important question. But I think the order of the words matters.

What we need is not only a theory of general intelligence. We need a general theory of intelligence.

A theory of general intelligence asks what would make an agent broadly capable. A general theory of intelligence asks what intelligence is as a phenomenon. It should explain intelligence wherever it appears: in animals, humans, machines, markets, institutions, scientific communities, and perhaps civilization as a whole. It should not begin by asking only how to build AGI. It should ask what structure allows any system to transform experience into useful models, use those models to anticipate the future, act through them, and improve them through feedback.

The distinction matters because intelligence is easy to define too loosely.

We often say intelligence is the ability to adapt. That is true, but it is not enough. A plant adapts. A thermostat adapts. Evolution adapts. A market adapts. If intelligence only means adaptation, then the word becomes so broad that it stops explaining anything.

A stronger definition has to explain how adaptation happens.

Every intelligent system faces the same basic problem: how to act in a world larger than its immediate perception. The world is dynamic. The future is uncertain. The system has limited energy, limited time, limited memory, and limited access to truth. It cannot know everything. It has to compress reality into something useful enough to guide action.

That means intelligence is not merely a property that a human, animal, machine, or civilization possesses. Intelligence is a loop.

At minimum, the loop has this shape:

1. Perceive signals from the world.
2. Represent those signals as internal structure.
3. Preserve useful structure across time as memory.
4. Predict what may happen next.
5. Assign value, meaning, danger, reward, or preference.
6. Act in the world.
7. Observe the consequence.
8. Compare expectation with reality.
9. Update the model.
10. Reuse the improved model in the future.

A system that only perceives does not yet understand. A system that only remembers does not yet act. A system that only predicts but never intervenes is incomplete. A system that acts but does not learn is just a mechanism.

Intelligence begins when perception, memory, prediction, action, and feedback become recursive.

This is why I keep returning to the structure of reality. The brain exists because reality is not given to us directly. An animal does not have access to the full structure of the world. It has local sensory input: light, sound, smell, pressure, hunger, pain. From these signals, the brain must infer where it is, what matters, what is changing, what can be ignored, what can be used, and what may happen next.

To be intelligent is to build a usable model of reality while being trapped inside reality.

The model is never complete. It does not need to be complete. It only needs to be good enough to guide action. A mouse does not need physics. It needs to know where food is, where danger is, and how to get home. A human does not perceive society directly. We build models of people, incentives, language, institutions, money, status, and time.

Intelligence is the ability to preserve useful structure from experience and act through it.

Memory is central because memory gives experience continuity. Without memory, every moment is isolated. There is no learning, no identity, no world model, no accumulation of structure. Memory allows the past to shape the present. Prediction allows the future to shape the present. Action connects both back to reality.

Modern AI has made prediction incredibly powerful. A language model predicts the next token. A vision model predicts labels or latent structure. A reinforcement learning agent predicts value. Prediction is everywhere because prediction is one of the central operations of intelligence.

But prediction alone is not intelligence.

A system can predict without caring. It can predict without acting. It can predict without understanding which errors matter. Intelligence requires value. Something must matter to the system, or to the process using the system. There must be a reason one future is preferred over another.

This is why my compressed definition is:

> Intelligence is a memory-guided prediction-action loop under value constraints.

Memory gives continuity.

Prediction gives anticipation.

Action gives agency.

Value gives direction.

Feedback gives learning.

Remove any of these, and the loop breaks.

This framing should apply across substrates. A biological brain is one implementation. It uses neurons, bodies, senses, drives, emotions, and movement. An AI agent is another implementation. It uses data, parameters, context, tools, memory, objectives, and feedback. A market is another kind of distributed intelligence. It stores preferences and scarcity in prices, updates through transactions, and reallocates resources through feedback. A civilization is also an intelligence loop. It perceives through science, stores memory through language and institutions, predicts through models, acts through technology and policy, and learns through history, failure, and transmission.

The substrate changes. The loop remains.

This is the reason a general theory of intelligence cannot belong only to neuroscience, or only to machine learning, or only to philosophy. It has to describe the structure that allows intelligence to exist in any medium. That structure is not a single algorithm. It is an organization of processes: representation, memory, prediction, valuation, action, feedback, learning, transfer, and self-modeling.

The loop becomes more powerful when the system begins to model itself. A simple agent models the world. A more advanced agent models itself as part of the world. It can ask: What do I know? What do I not know? What are my limits? What happens if I act? How will others respond to me? How should I change myself to perform better next time?

Self-modeling turns intelligence inward.

This matters because the agent is not outside reality. It is embedded in the same world it is trying to understand. Its body, sensors, memory, tools, social position, and goals all shape what it can know and what it can do. A system that models reality but not itself will eventually misunderstand its own actions. A system that models both reality and itself can become recursive.

Structure of Reality began with a question: What is the structure of reality?

But the deeper question is: what kind of system can discover that structure?

This is why the nature of intelligence matters to me. I am not only interested in artificial intelligence as a technology. I am interested in intelligence as a general phenomenon: something that appears in animals, humans, machines, language, science, markets, institutions, and perhaps civilization as a whole.

I am a Chinese American computer scientist navigating toward the origin of intelligence.

This journal is my notebook for that journey.

I want to understand how intelligence emerges, how it is represented, how it learns, how it acts, how it preserves knowledge, and how it can be built in machines. Computation is my path into this question. But the question is larger than computation alone.

The question is how reality becomes structured inside an agent, and how that structure allows the agent to act back upon reality.

A general theory of intelligence should not merely say that intelligence is adaptation. It should explain how adaptation happens. It should explain how experience becomes memory, how memory becomes prediction, how prediction becomes action, how action produces feedback, and how feedback improves the model.

My current formulation is simple:

> Intelligence is recursive adaptive modeling in service of goal-directed action.

Or even shorter:

> Intelligence is a memory-guided prediction-action loop under value constraints.

A theory of general intelligence asks how an agent can become broadly capable. A general theory of intelligence asks how any system becomes capable at all.

The second question is more fundamental.

If the structure of reality is what intelligence is trying to discover, then a general theory of intelligence is the theory of how that discovery becomes possible.
